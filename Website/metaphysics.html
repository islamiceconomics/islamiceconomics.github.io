What This Book Is

Islamic economics has long been pulled in two directions. On one side stands a body of classical jurisprudence with extraordinary conceptual depth — rizq as divinely apportioned provision, barakah as multiplicative blessing, riba as a structural pathology, qadar as the actualization of the Unseen, niyyah as the determinant of the moral status of any act. On the other side stands a modern technical literature that has largely reduced Islamic economics to a set of contractual prohibitions: no interest, no gharar, no maysir, plus zakat and waqf. The classical concepts are then either left as pious decoration or quietly dropped.

This volume argues that the classical concepts can be articulated rigorously — but only if we are prepared to use the conceptual tools that have actually been developed for thinking about non-classical realities: the measurement problem in quantum mechanics, ergodicity in stochastic dynamics, analytical idealism in the philosophy of mind, non-computability in mathematics and theoretical physics, and the Akbarian metaphysical tradition itself. None of these by itself is enough. Together, they make it possible to take wujud, tajalli, rizq, and qadar seriously as load-bearing concepts in an economic theory rather than as ornaments.

The book is foundational. It does not give a new microeconomic model or a new monetary theory; those are the work of Volumes I–IV. It articulates the conceptual ground on which such theory can be built without the embarrassments that have characterized the discipline so far.

The Series

Foundations of Islamic Economic Theory — a five-volume work

Volume 0 · Metaphysical Foundations

Available now. The conceptual ground. Establishes the metaphysical and mathematical apparatus needed to articulate Islamic economic concepts rigorously. 274 pp.

Volume I · Microeconomic Theory

In preparation. Formal preference theory, choice under qadar, the consumer and the firm under Islamic constraints, general equilibrium, welfare economics with maqasid-based criteria. The Mas-Colell–Whinston–Green of Islamic microeconomics.

Volume II · Macroeconomics & Public Finance

Planned. Aggregate dynamics, monetary economics under a non-interest-based system, fiscal theory of zakat and waqf, business cycles, growth.

Volume III · Islamic Finance Theory

Planned. Asset pricing without riba, contract theory of mudarabah and musharakah, risk-sharing under uncertainty, sukuk and Islamic capital markets.

Volume IV · Comparative Economic Systems

Planned. Islamic economics in dialogue with capitalism, socialism, and the heterodox traditions. Empirical contrasts, institutional comparisons, normative evaluation.

Conceptual Foundations

Five contemporary thinkers whose work supplies the apparatus this volume builds on

Volume 0 does not invent its conceptual machinery from scratch. It draws on five rigorous bodies of work — none of them written from within the Islamic tradition, all of them indispensable for articulating that tradition properly. Each is treated explicitly in the book; each appears here so the reader can see where the apparatus comes from.

⟨ψ|

Sean Carroll — Quantum Foundations

Theoretical physicist (Johns Hopkins, Santa Fe Institute). Leading contemporary defender of the Everett (Many-Worlds) interpretation of quantum mechanics. Used in: Chapter 5 (the measurement problem and live interpretations) and Chapter 16 (resolution of the determinism / qadar tension). Carroll's case that classical Newtonian determinism was abandoned by physicists themselves a century ago is the entry point into the volume's treatment of agency.

⟨X⟩

Ole Peters — Ergodicity Economics

Theoretical physicist (London Mathematical Laboratory). Founder of ergodicity economics, which distinguishes time-averaged growth from ensemble-averaged expected value and overturns much of decision theory in the process. Used in: Chapter 6 (the time–ensemble distinction), Chapter 12 (halal and haram as distinct wealth dynamics), Chapter 13 (riba as financial entropy), Chapter 17 (the architecture of permanence).

∞

Bernardo Kastrup — Analytical Idealism

Philosopher and computer scientist. Develops a contemporary form of objective idealism (Mind-at-Large; dissociated alters) that takes the hard problem of consciousness seriously. Used in: Chapter 7 (the ontology of mind), Chapter 9 (the structural correspondence with tawhid and wahdat al-wujud). Provides a non-Islamic articulation of the structural shape of the Akbarian position.

∂

Roger Penrose — Non-Computability

Mathematical physicist (Oxford), Nobel laureate. His arguments from Gödel's incompleteness theorems and the halting problem to the non-algorithmic character of mathematical understanding underwrite a strong sense in which niyyah (intention) is not a computational state. Used in: Chapter 8 (non-computability and the reality of intention), and the foundational role of niyyah in Chapter 18.

و

Ibn ʿArabi · Mulla Sadra · Shah Wali Allah

The Akbarian metaphysical tradition itself: wahdat al-wujud, tajalli, the divine names, ʿalam al-mithal, insan al-kamil. Treated systematically rather than ornamentally. Used in: Chapter 9 (the central exposition), and woven through Parts III and IV. The volume's claim is not that these doctrines need quantum mechanics to be true, but that contemporary apparatus makes their structure newly legible.

How to Read This Book

If you are an economist: begin with Chapter 1 (the two failures the book is responding to), skim Chapter 2 (the methodological taxonomy of "levels of rigor"), then proceed to Part II for the apparatus and Part III for the worked treatments.

If you are a physicist or mathematician: Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 will be familiar; the payoff is in Part III, where the apparatus is applied to riba, rizq, barakah, and qadar. Chapter 13's collapse theorem and Chapter 11's super-linear scaling result are probably the most technically substantive passages.

If you come from the Islamic scholarly tradition: Chapter 9 is the natural starting point, followed by the worked chapters in Part III. Part II can then be read as needed when those chapters cite the apparatus.

If you are a general reader: read the Preface, Chapter 1, then sample any chapter from Part III that catches your eye. Chapter 14 (gratitude and the multiplication of wealth) and Chapter 15 (barakah) are the most accessible entry points.
