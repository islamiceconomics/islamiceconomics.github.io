## LinkedIn

The Ottoman Public Debt Administration seized control of Ottoman state revenues in 1881. A century later, the IMF began offering structural adjustment programs to dozens of countries, many of them Muslim-majority nations. The pattern seems familiar: foreign creditors gaining control over domestic policy. But the reality is more complex than simple predation. IMF loans come at below-market rates. South Korea used its 1997 program as a bridge to genuine reform and never needed to return. Pakistan tells a different story—24 IMF programs since 1958, each time promising structural changes that never come. The debt trap isn't just about predatory lending. It's about the willingness of both borrowers and lenders to repeat cycles that serve neither economic development nor citizen welfare. Sometimes the failure is as much about domestic governance as external pressure. Listen: https://islamiceconomics.github.io/podcast.html#ep-be-2

### Voiceover

In 1881, European creditors seized Ottoman state revenues through the Public Debt Administration. Today, the IMF offers structural adjustment programs with similar control mechanisms. But here's what's interesting: South Korea took one IMF program in 1997, implemented painful but genuine reforms, and graduated permanently. Pakistan has cycled through twenty-four programs since 1958, each time promising structural changes that never materialize. The debt trap isn't just about predatory lending. It's about what happens after the money arrives. Sometimes the failure is as much about domestic governance as external pressure.
