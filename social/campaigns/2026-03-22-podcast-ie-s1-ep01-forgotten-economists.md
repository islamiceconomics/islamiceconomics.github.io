## LinkedIn

The story of Islamic economics begins not in modern boardrooms, but in the bustling markets of 7th-century Mecca. Before his prophetic mission, Muhammad (PBUH) was a successful merchant. This wasn't incidental—it shaped how early Islamic civilization approached trade, wealth, and economic justice. By the 14th century, Ibn Khaldun had articulated supply and demand, the division of labor, and labor theory of value. Four centuries before Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations. Scholars like Abu Yusuf and Al-Ghazali developed sophisticated frameworks around riba prohibition, zakat distribution, and market regulation. These weren't abstract theories—they governed trade networks spanning from Al-Andalus to Southeast Asia. Today's Islamic finance revival draws from this deep well of economic thought, adapting centuries-old principles to modern markets.
