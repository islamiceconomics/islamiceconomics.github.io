During the caliphate of Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, zakat collection became so effective that administrators reportedly struggled to find eligible recipients in parts of the empire. This wasn't charity—it was systematic fiscal policy. Zakat operates as a 2.5% annual wealth tax on assets above a minimum threshold, functioning as both automatic stabilizer and redistributive mechanism. Modern estimates suggest that if properly collected across the Muslim world, it could generate $200-600 billion annually—enough to eliminate extreme poverty in Muslim-majority countries without foreign borrowing. Countries like Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan are now experimenting with systematic zakat collection, rediscovering principles that worked thirteen centuries ago. The question isn't whether zakat can work as macroeconomic policy, but why modern states haven't fully embraced what their predecessors proved effective.
