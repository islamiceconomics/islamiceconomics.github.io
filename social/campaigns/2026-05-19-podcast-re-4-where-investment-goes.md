## LinkedIn

Two countries with identical savings rates can have vastly different growth outcomes. The difference? How their financial systems allocate capital. Capital misallocation reduces productivity in developing countries by 30 to 50 percent. Islamic economics recognizes this challenge and offers a theoretical solution: ban interest-based lending that favors safe borrowers with mediocre projects. Instead, Islamic finance promotes equity-based financing through mudaraba and musharaka—structures that should align incentives and direct capital toward genuinely productive uses. But theory and practice diverge sharply. Most Islamic banks rely on murabaha, which functions economically like conventional lending. Genuine profit-loss sharing remains rare. The result? Muslim-majority economies still suffer from massive capital misallocation, particularly in real estate markets. The promise of redirected capital flows remains largely unfulfilled.
